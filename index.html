What is a bacteriology LIS?

A Laboratory Information System (LIS) is the software a hospital laboratory uses to keep track of patient samples and their results. The bacteriology section has a harder job than most. A blood culture is not a one-hour test. It is incubated for days, checked on a schedule, stained, identified, and tested against antibiotics before a final result can be released.

This system is built around that slow, careful process, so nothing is lost between shifts and every step has a name, a time and a person attached to it.

Why I built it

I work in this laboratory every day. I know where specimens wait, where paper gets lost and which reading is easy to miss at the end of a long shift. I am not a software engineer, so I used AI tools to turn that experience into working software, while I decided what the system should do and checked every screen against real bench practice.

The specimen journey

Every specimen moves through the same six stages, and the dashboard always shows which one it is in.

  1. Registered

    The specimen is logged with patient, source and ward. The system creates the accession number, label, barcode and QR code.

  2. Incubating

    Culture bottles go into the automated incubator. Timers count down to the next reading and raise an alarm when one is due.

  3. For Workup

    Growth is examined. Gram stain findings are recorded and the organism is worked up for identification.

  4. For AST

    Antimicrobial susceptibility testing shows which antibiotics work against the organism.

  5. For Verification

    A second set of eyes reviews everything. Nothing leaves the laboratory unchecked.

  6. Released

    The verified result is released and the record is closed, with the full history kept.

Inside the system

A dashboard for the whole section

The first screen answers the questions a bench technologist asks every morning: how many specimens are pending, how many are incubating, whether any alarms are active and what was released today.

  • Live counts for pending, incubating and awaiting verification
  • An active specimen panel with its current stage, Gram stain and incubation status
  • A specimen queue with a single button to continue the next step

Registration with automatic labels

Registering a specimen takes one form. The accession number, label, barcode and QR code are generated automatically, which removes handwriting and typing errors at the very start of the process.

  • Unique accession numbers in a consistent format
  • Printable labels with barcode and QR code
  • Scan station to pull up a specimen in seconds

Incubation timers that do not forget

Cultures are incubated in the automated blood culture system at 35 to 37 °C. Each bottle has its own timer, due date and countdown, so the next reading is never a matter of memory.

  • Active timers listed by time remaining
  • Due dates and times for final incubation
  • Alarms when a reading is overdue

One record for every specimen

Each specimen has a single record that gathers its Gram stain, identification and susceptibility results in one place, with the stage it is in always visible at the top.

  • Gram stain recording
  • Organism identification and AST results
  • Complete history of who did what and when

Verification and reports

Results wait in a verification queue until they are reviewed. Once released, the reports module collects the numbers a section head needs.

  • A clear queue of results awaiting review
  • Reports for released and registered specimens
  • Fewer loose papers and fewer repeated questions

Accounts and roles

Staff sign in with their own accounts, and what they can do depends on their role. Administrators manage users and settings from a separate area.

  • Individual sign-in for every staff member
  • Role-based access, with an Administrator area
  • Global search by accession number or patient

AI wrote the code. I wrote the requirements. I described each screen the way a technologist would, tested it against real specimen scenarios, and sent it back until it matched how the bench actually works. The result is software shaped by someone who uses it, not a generic template.
